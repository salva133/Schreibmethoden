# Advice from Famous Authors

---

## Stephen King

> "If you want to be a writer, you must do two things above all others: read a lot and write a lot."

> "Write with the door closed, rewrite with the door open."

> "Kill your darlings, even when it breaks your egocentric little scribbler's heart."

> "The first draft of a book, even a long one, should take no more than three months, the length of a season."

Core principle: **discipline over inspiration.** Write every day, do not wait for a muse.

*Source: Stephen King, On Writing: A Memoir of the Craft (2000)*

---

## Ernest Hemingway

> "There is no great writing, only great rewriting." *(attributed)*

> "Write drunk, edit sober." *(attributed; the principle: let go first, filter afterwards)*

Core principle: **reduction.** The iceberg principle: seven eighths of the story stay below the surface, but the foundation carries the weight.

*Source: Ernest Hemingway, Death in the Afternoon (1932), iceberg theory; quotes from interviews and essays*

---

## Anton Chekhov

> "If in the first act you have hung a pistol on the wall, then in the following one it should be fired."

Core principle: **Chekhov's gun.** Every element of the story has to do its work. Nothing is decoration.

*Source: S. Shchukin, Memoirs (1911), the earliest written record of the line*

## George Orwell

Six rules for clear writing:

1. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
2. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
4. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

*Source: George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946)*

## William Faulkner

> "Kill your darlings."

*Source: William Faulkner, lecture and interview material; the "kill your darlings" line is alternatively attributed to Arthur Quiller-Couch.*

## Elmore Leonard

Ten rules of good writing (excerpt):

- Never open a book with weather.
- Avoid prologues.
- Avoid detailed descriptions of characters.
- Don't go into great detail describing places and things.
- Try to leave out the part that readers tend to skip.

> "If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it."

*Source: Elmore Leonard, "Easy on the Adverbs, Exclamation Points and Especially Hooptedoodle", New York Times (2001); later published as 10 Rules of Writing (2007)*

## Kurt Vonnegut

Eight rules of storytelling:

1. Use the time of a total stranger in such a way that he or she will not feel the time was wasted.
2. Give the reader at least one character he or she can root for.
3. Every character should want something, even if it is only a glass of water.
4. Every sentence must do one of two things: reveal character or advance the action.
5. Start as close to the end as possible.
6. Be a sadist. No matter how sweet and innocent your leading characters, make awful things happen to them, so that the reader may see what they are made of.
7. Write to please just one person. If you open a window and make love to the world, so to speak, your story will get pneumonia.
8. Give your readers as much information as possible as soon as possible. To hell with suspense.

*Source: Kurt Vonnegut, preface to Bagombo Snuff Box (1999)*

## Brandon Sanderson

Sanderson's teaching is the working school of modern genre fiction. Three concepts stand out.

**Promise, progress, payoff.** Every story promises something: genre, tone, conflict. It shows progress towards that promise. It pays it off. Whoever opens as a crime novel and ends as a romance has broken the promise.

**The three axes of a character.** Likability, proactivity, competence. A character does not need all three, but at least one has to be strong, otherwise the reader lets go.

**Yes, but / No, and.** Scene outcomes that drive the plot on. "Yes" and "No" are dead, "Yes, but" and "No, and" are alive.

Sanderson's writing lectures are freely available (BYU, brandonsanderson.com) and, at 13 and 16 sessions respectively, the most thorough free writing course on the net.

*Sources: Brandon Sanderson, Writing Lectures (2020, 2025); Mistborn (2006); The Way of Kings (2010)*

## Ursula K. Le Guin

Le Guin writes about craft like a poet who happens to produce novels. *Steering the Craft* (1998) is the best style school for narrative prose.

Core topics:

**Sound.** Sentences have rhythm whether the author wants them to or not. Anyone who cannot hear how his prose sounds is writing deaf. Reading aloud is an obligation, not a tip.

**Mix sentence lengths.** Long sentences carry a thought. Short ones land. A run of sentences of equal length is dead, however fine each one is on its own.

**Take POV seriously.** Le Guin is sharp about head-hopping and blurred focalization. Whoever changes perspective should know that he is doing it.

> "The sound of the language is where it all begins and what it all comes back to."

*Source: Ursula K. Le Guin, Steering the Craft (1998, revised edition 2015)*

---

## Ray Bradbury

> "You must stay drunk on writing so reality cannot destroy you."

> "Quantity gives experience. From experience alone can quality come."

Core principle: **energy over correctness.** Bradbury recommended starting a short story every day, finishing one a week, fifty a year, and at least one of them will not be terrible. Volume beats caution.

*Source: Ray Bradbury, Zen in the Art of Writing (1990)*

## Gustav Freytag

The German classic of dramaturgy. Freytag's pyramid is the continental European root of the three-act structure and applies above all to classical tragedy.

**Five stages:**

1. **Exposition:** introduction of characters, world, starting situation.
2. **Rising action:** the conflict develops, the hero pursues his goal.
3. **Climax:** the decisive moment, often in the third act of a five-act drama.
4. **Falling action:** the consequences unfold, retarding moments create a last surge of tension.
5. **Catastrophe or resolution:** in tragedy the fall, in comedy the clearing up.

Freytag analyzed drama, not the novel. His model is more symmetrical than the modern three-act structure, whose climax sits further back. For classical material and for the analysis of German classicism (Schiller, Lessing, Hebbel) Freytag remains the standard.

*Source: Gustav Freytag, Die Technik des Dramas (1863), in English Technique of the Drama*

## General Wisdom

> **Reading is how the craft is learned.** An author who does not read is like a locksmith who never looks at a lock.

> **Writing is learned only by writing.** Not from books about it, but from the pages.
