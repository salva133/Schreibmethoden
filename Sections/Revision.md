# Revision & Editing

---
## The First Draft Is Raw, and That Is as It Should Be

Let the first draft go cold: do not look at it for **at least 2-4 weeks**. Then read it with fresh eyes.

The goal of a first draft is **existence**, not quality. Write first, improve afterwards.

> "The first draft of anything is shit."
> *Ernest Hemingway (attributed)*

---
## Several Revision Passes

Work the manuscript in *separate passes*, one per level:

| Pass | Focus |
|-----------|-------|
| **1. Macro edit** | Structure, plot, character arcs, pacing, missing scenes |
| **2. Scene edit** | Every scene: goal? conflict? change? necessary? |
| **3. Style edit** | Sentence rhythm, word choice, redundancies, strength of the verbs |
| **4. Proofreading** | Spelling, grammar, punctuation |

**Source:** This structure appears among others in James Scott Bell, *Revision and Self-Editing for Publication* (2008).

---
## Self-Editing Tips

- **Read aloud.** Ears hear the stumbling points that eyes read past.
- **Read backwards** (sentence by sentence, from the end) for proofreading. That breaks the flow of reading.
- **Change font and size.** The brain sees the text as new.
- **Keep a personal error list.** Every author has pet phrases and weak spots.
- **Kill your darlings** (King/Faulkner): cut the passages you love that do not move the story forward.

---
## A Correction Must Not Introduce a New Error

When a word is swapped out because of a style rule (repetition, filter word, adverb, tense), putting in *any* replacement is not enough. Three checks on the new sentence before the correction stands:

- **Semantically sound.** Does the image still hold? "Making the shadows look older" tips over, because shadows have no perceptible age. If the replacement breaks the image it is wrong, however neatly it solves the repetition.
- **Grammatically compatible.** Number, gender, case and tense have to fit the new referent. A term carried over from an earlier version brings its old form with it (the singular "every shadow" does not fit the plural "than they were").
- **Replace the right end.** With a repeated word the reflex is to swap the *first*, more prominent occurrence. Often the quieter solution is to leave the *second* one standing and rebuild the first, or the other way round. Test both directions; the one with less collateral damage wins.

## Scene-List Diagnosis

Before the first editing pass, keep a table of all scenes. Columns: scene number, POV, the character's goal, conflict, outcome, function in the plot.

The result often reads alarmingly. Scenes without conflict stand out at once. Scenes without an outcome (the situation at the end is the same as at the start) become visible. Scenes without a plot function give themselves away.

Rule of thumb: a scene without conflict or without an outcome is not a scene but a transition. Transitions are to be summarized, not played out.

The method is the most effective macro-edit diagnosis there is and costs half a day per manuscript.

## Sanderson's Draft System

An alternative to the phase-oriented table: one defined goal per pass instead of one level of granularity.

| Draft | Focus |
|-------|-------|
| **1. First draft** | Let it exist. Pace, no vanity. |
| **2. Character pass** | Characters only. Do they hold up? Do they grow? Is the voice consistent? |
| **3. Setting pass** | World, sensory impressions, consistency of places and rules. |
| **4. Plot pass** | Setup and payoff, pinch points, subplot connections. |
| **5. Style pass** | Sentence rhythm, sharpness of verbs, style rules. |
| **6. Proofreading** | Spelling, punctuation. |

Passes can be combined once the manuscript is stable. With bigger weaknesses, separate passes pay off. An editing pass that tries everything at once sees nothing properly.

Sanderson's system supplements the phase-oriented table, it does not replace it. Phases sort by depth of damage, passes by subject.

*Source: Brandon Sanderson, Writing Lectures #11 (brandonsanderson.com)*

## Continuity Check

A pass of its own, before proofreading. Eye colors, names, family relations, injuries, timelines, weekdays, weather, clothing across scenes.

In practice: keep a table with character, eye color, hair color, height, marks (scars, tattoos), key dates. For world elements: rules of magic, level of technology, geography.

The most common errors in a first draft: an injury in scene 12 that has vanished by scene 14. A journey that takes three days according to act I and is done in one day in act II. A brother who is older in chapter 3 and younger in chapter 18.

Beta readers often find such errors, but they cost trust. Better to clear them out beforehand.

## Sensitivity Reading

For material that is delicate historically, culturally or in terms of lived experience: a reading by someone close to the group or the experience depicted. Not as an instance of censorship but as a corrective for blind spots.

Cases: depictions of illness, disability, migration, religious practice, ethnic identity, trauma, professional milieus one has not worked in oneself.

Sensitivity reading does not replace research. It checks whether the research actually arrives in the prose, or whether clichés, omissions and wrong notes have been left standing.

The sensitivity reader judges, the author decides. Not every note has to be taken. Recurring notes should be taken seriously.

## Getting Feedback

1. **Beta readers:** first readers from the target audience who give honest feedback on content.
2. **Writing group:** regular exchange with other authors.
3. **Professional editing:** worth it only after several rounds of your own revision.

**Important with feedback:** Do not accept every criticism. Look for patterns: when several independent readers are bothered by the same thing, it is a real problem.

**Sorting feedback, in practice.** Keep a table, one row per piece of feedback, one column per reader. When three beta readers name the same point independently, it is a structural problem. When only one names it, it may be taste. The diagonal case (one reader sees the problem, others explicitly disagree) is the hardest position. Check your own view, get a fourth opinion.

The solution a beta reader proposes is almost always wrong. The place where he stumbles is almost always correctly identified. Listen to the problem, not to the proposed fix.

> "Remember: when people tell you something's wrong or doesn't work for them, they're almost always right. When they tell you exactly what they think is wrong and how to fix it, they're almost always wrong."
> *Neil Gaiman*
