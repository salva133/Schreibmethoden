# Writing Techniques & Figures of Speech

---

## Show, Don't Tell

The single most important rule in modern storytelling.

**Principle:** Instead of *telling* the reader what a character feels or is, *show* it through action, body language, dialogue and sensory detail.

**Example:**

| ❌ Tell | ✅ Show |
|---------|---------|
| "He was angry." | "He threw the cup at the wall. The shards rang off the stove." |
| "She was nervous." | "Her fingers crumbled the paper, folded it, folded it again." |
| "The village was poor." | "The children ran barefoot over the dusty road, their ribs showing under their shirts." |

**Use:**
- Show emotions through *body language and action*
- Show character traits through *decisions*, not through description
- Let conflicts live through *dialogue*
- Create atmosphere through *sensory detail* (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch)

**Important:** Not an absolute law. Every novel needs a balance of show and tell. Short passages of tell can create pace.

**Sources:** Anton Chekhov (origin of the principle); Lajos Egri, *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946); James N. Frey, *How to Write a Damn Good Novel* (1987)

---

## When Tell Is Right

Show is the basic form, but tell has functions of its own. Show everything and you tire the reader out and give away your pace.

**Transitions and jumps in time.** "Three weeks later he was fit to ride again." A full scenic treatment of the recovery would be waste. Tell compresses.

**Incidental information.** "She was a doctor." Showing such facts dramatically would overemphasize them. When the information matters to the plot later, it gets redeemed in a scene. Until then the report is enough.

**Inner life at an evaluating distance.** First person, retrospective: "I was young and stupid then, and I thought that was love." Here tell is voice, not a shortcoming.

**Pace after a show scene.** After an intense, sensory chapter, a brief paragraph of tell can regulate the reader's breathing. Show without pauses of tell becomes hard work.

**Results instead of process.** "The negotiation lasted four hours. At the end he had what he wanted." When the negotiation itself has no bearing on character or plot, it needs no scene.

Rule of thumb: when the event is emotionally central or exposes the character, show it. When it carries information or bridges time, tell it.

## Sensory Writing: The Five Senses

Deep immersion arises when the reader feels that *all five senses* are addressed:

- **Sight:** light, colors, movement, detail
- **Hearing:** sounds, silence, rhythm, voice
- **Smell:** often underrated. Smells set off powerful emotional memories
- **Touch:** temperature, texture, pain, pressure
- **Taste:** meals, fear (a metallic taste), kisses

**A hierarchy of the senses.** Sight and hearing dominate in most texts. Smell and touch are underrepresented and therefore hit harder when they do appear. Smell above all, because it is wired directly to memory.

**No checklist of senses.** Do not tick off all five senses in one paragraph. A scene does not get richer because the author dutifully stages "saw, heard, smelled, felt". Two or three sensory impressions are enough when they are concrete and fit the mood of the scene.

**Perception colored by POV.** A tired character notices different things from a rested one. A hungry one sees bread before faces. A suspicious one hears footsteps before voices. Sensory impressions characterize when they are filtered.

## In Medias Res: Into the Middle of Things

The story begins in the middle of a scene or a conflict, not at the start of the chain of events.

**Effect:** Creates tension and curiosity at once. The reader asks himself: *how did it come to this?*

**Familiar examples:** Many thrillers and action novels open with a chase, a fight or a crisis before the backstory is explained.

**Risk: the reader with no ground under him.** Without an anchor the conflict makes no sense. Whoever starts in the middle of a fight has to make clear within a few paragraphs who is fighting whom and why. Otherwise the scene reads as violence with nothing at stake. Minimum context: who the character is, what he wants, what is on the line.

**Drip-feeding the backstory.** After the opening, the backstory is let in drop by drop, never as a block. A memory here, a subordinate clause there. The reader builds the gaps himself, and that is part of the pull.

**Relation to foreshadowing.** The opening scene often raises questions that are redeemed later through setup and payoff. A seemingly incidental detail in the opening can become the decisive element.

## Stream of Consciousness

A character's immediate thoughts and perceptions are rendered unfiltered and associatively. Long sentences, emotion dominating over action.

**Suited to:** intense interiority, trauma, psychological depth, a character's confusion or exhaustion.

**Known practitioners:** Virginia Woolf (*Mrs Dalloway*), James Joyce (*Ulysses*), William Faulkner (*The Sound and the Fury*)

## Perspective & Point of View (POV)

The choice of narrative perspective determines the emotional closeness to the story:

| Perspective | Description | Effect |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| **First person** | Narrator = protagonist | Maximum closeness, intimacy |
| **Close third person** | "He/she thought…" | Closeness plus narrative flexibility |
| **Alternating POVs** | Several characters in turn | Complex plots, drama through the reader knowing more |
| **Omniscient narrator** | A view of everything | Epic distance, good for large arcs |

**Tip:** Do *not* change narrative perspective in the middle of a scene (avoid head-hopping).

**Psychic distance (John Gardner).** Within one POV there are degrees of closeness, from external report to direct stream of consciousness:

1. "It was the winter of 1853. Wilhelm Marten went to work." (report, distant)
2. "Wilhelm Marten went to work on a cold winter day." (closer)
3. "Wilhelm was freezing. He trudged across the iced-over cobbles." (closer third person)
4. "Damned frost. Wilhelm pulled his coat higher." (thought, unmarked)
5. "Damned frost. When was this going to stop?" (free indirect monologue)

A scene gains depth when it moves between these levels. Start at a distance, then closer as the emotional heat rises.

**Slipping POV.** Perspective shifts that creep in during a scene read as technical sloppiness, even when the reader cannot name them. When a character suddenly knows what another one feels, the perspective has slipped. Either show the feeling through expression and tone of voice, or accept the break in POV and end the scene.

**Focalization (Genette).** *Who narrates* and *who perceives* are separate. An omniscient narrator can focalize through the eyes of a single character without being that character's voice. Important for narratives that want narrative distance and figural depth at the same time.

*Source: John Gardner, The Art of Fiction (1983); Gérard Genette, Discours du récit (1972)*

## Building Tension: Techniques

- **Cliffhanger:** End a chapter on an open conflict, a question or a threat.
- **Foreshadowing:** Scatter early hints of later events.
- **Dramatic irony:** The reader knows something the character does not.
- **Time pressure:** A deadline creates tension automatically.
- **Contrast:** Quiet scenes after action and the other way round strengthen both states.
- **Riddles and open questions:** Keep the reader curious through unanswered questions.

## Writing Dialogue

- Dialogue should *always* serve a function: show character, carry out a conflict or convey information.
- Dialogue sounds natural when characters **talk past each other**, interrupt or evade.
- Avoid "talking heads". Always anchor dialogue in action, body language or detail of the room.
- Different characters speak *differently*: vocabulary, rhythm, tics.
- Avoid too many synonyms for "said". "Said" is invisible; "gasped", "shouted", "whispered" only where they are really needed.

**Action beats instead of dialogue tags.** Instead of hanging a "he said" on every line, scatter small actions that show character and room at the same time.

> "You could have told me that sooner."
> She unscrewed the lid of the thermos. For a moment the steam hid her face.
> "I tried."

Action beats anchor dialogue in the body, replace tags and carry subtext (she needs the steam to hide behind).

**Subtext.** Characters rarely say directly what they mean. An argument about the washing-up is an argument about neglect. A question about the time is a request to leave. Subtext arises when the author knows the real negotiation and writes only the surface.

**The dialogue triangle.** Two characters talk about a third thing: another person, an event, an object. The third thing is what actually carries the tension, because values and loyalties become visible through it without the characters going at each other directly.

**Avoid "as you know" dialogue.** "As you know, brother, our father has been dead three years." Nobody talks like that. Information both characters already have does not belong in their dialogue. When the reader needs it, bring it in another way: a memory, an observation, a letter.

**Many voices.** Accent or dialect not through phonetics ("ah'm gonna tell ya summat now") but through syntax, word choice and subject matter. A character who builds short sentences and never talks about feelings sounds different from one who thinks in nested clauses and constantly alludes to books. No apostrophes, no spelled-out sound effects.

**Sources:** Elmore Leonard, *10 Rules of Writing* (2007); Stephen King, *On Writing* (2000); Robert McKee, *Dialogue* (2016)

## Figurative Language & Figures of Speech

| Figure | Explanation | Example |
|------------|-----------|---------|
| **Metaphor** | Direct equation | "Life is a battlefield." |
| **Simile** | Comparison using "like" or "as" | "His silence was heavy as lead." |
| **Personification** | Things act like people | "The wind complained through the cracks." |
| **Alliteration** | Matching initial sounds | "Steel, silence, shadow" |
| **Hyperbole** | Overstatement | "He had waited an eternity." |
| **Understatement** | Deliberate downplaying | After the catastrophe: "It didn't go entirely to plan." |
| **Anaphora** | Repetition at the start of a sentence | "We fought. We lost. We stayed." |
| **Chiasmus** | Crosswise arrangement | "He wrote to live, and he lived to write." |
| **Parallelism** | Identical sentence structure | "She came without luggage. She left without a word." |
| **Synecdoche** | Part for the whole | "All hands on deck" for sailors, "keel" for ship |
| **Metonymy** | A term replaced by a related one | "Berlin is silent." (for the German government) |

**Warning: figures of speech show.** A figure of speech that is noticed is a broken one. Once the reader sees that this is an anaphora, it has lost its purpose. Use figures sparingly, at key moments, or the text sounds like a speech or a style exercise.

## Clustering & Mind Mapping

**Clustering** is a creative preparation technique: a word or concept is written in the middle, then every thought branches out around it associatively, without judgment, without censorship.

**Uses:** finding ideas, developing characters, searching for themes and motifs, breaking through writer's block.

**Source:** Gabriele Lusser Rico, *Writing the Natural Way* (1983), who established clustering as a method for creative writing.
