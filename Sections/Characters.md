# Writing Characters

Characters carry a story. Plot is what happens. Characters are the reason we care. The sections below do not describe templates but attitudes: how do you approach a role so that it stops being a function and starts to breathe?

---

## The Protagonist

The protagonist is not the hero. He is the lens through which the reader sees the world. Which means: he may be blind, cowardly, wrong. But he has to *want*. Without wanting there is no story.

**Separate the inner and the outer goal.**
In *Story* (1997) Robert McKee distinguishes between conscious desire and unconscious need. What the protagonist *says* he wants is rarely what he *needs*. The most interesting characters carry that contradiction inside them, and the audience feels the tension even when the character is blind to it. McKee: *"What he believes he wants is the antithesis of what he actually but unwittingly wants."*

**The ghost.**
In *The Anatomy of Story* (2007) John Truby calls it the "ghost": an event from the past that haunts the protagonist in the present. It is not mere backstory but an open wound that warps his decisions. The ghost does not have to be explained. It should be palpable in the protagonist's reactions, in what he avoids, in what he cannot talk about.

**Dimensions instead of traits.**
McKee describes depth of character as *dimensions*: contradictions in the character's nature. A cowardly man capable of courage. A loving father who coldly manipulates other people. The more genuine contradictions a character carries, the more alive he seems.

**Mistakes with consequences.**
The protagonist has to make mistakes that damage the action, not just social gaffes. A man who never really fails does not change. And a protagonist without change is a character without an arc.

**A personal style of perception.**
In limited third person the protagonist filters everything. He notices what suits him and overlooks what is inconvenient. Two characters in the same scene do not see the same scene. That filter is characterization by omission.

**Sympathy is not likability.**
A protagonist does not have to be nice. He has to be *understandable*. In his writing lectures (2025) Brandon Sanderson distinguishes three axes: sympathy (likability), proactivity and competence. A protagonist can be low on all three scales as long as he is strong enough on at least one to hold the reader.

*Sources: Robert McKee, Story (1997); John Truby, The Anatomy of Story (2007); Brandon Sanderson, Writing Lectures (brandonsanderson.com, 2025)*

## The Love Interest

The love interest is not a reward and not an accessory. Write one that way and you feel the result: a character who functions but does not live.

**A story of her own, a goal of her own.**
The love interest has to be able to exist independently of the protagonist. Jenna Moreci puts it bluntly: *"Love interests that do not have lives outside of their story's protagonists aren't characters at all, they are objects."* The test: strike this character's romantic function, and is she still relevant to the action? If not, she is underdeveloped.

**Conflict through difference, not through misunderstanding.**
The weakest form of romantic tension is the misunderstanding that a single conversation would clear up. Real conflict arises when two people want something real that is mutually exclusive: different worlds, different values, different wounds.

**The love interest changes the protagonist, but not through relationship advice.**
The effect is subtler: a different way of seeing that the protagonist cannot ignore. A decision he regrets because he knows the other person would have done it differently. Not instruction but contact.

**Chemistry through the concrete.**
Romantic tension does not come from descriptions of feelings but from specific detail. How somebody laughs. A particular sentence at a particular moment. What the protagonist *does not* do because the other person is watching.

**A moment of glory of her own.**
The love interest needs at least one moment of shining independently of the relationship: a decision, an ability, a stance that shows why this character is interesting.

*Sources: Jenna Moreci, "10 Best Tips for Writing a Strong Love Interest" (jennamoreci.com); Well-Storied, "The Do's and Don'ts of Crafting Your Story's Love Interest" (well-storied.com)*

## Secondary Characters

Secondary characters are not decor and not carriers of information. They are the world. Through them it becomes palpable that something exists outside the protagonist.

**Every secondary character wants something.**
Even in the smallest appearance. A barkeeper who is impatient. A soldier who is afraid and hides it. That wanting does not have to be spelled out, but the author has to know it. Otherwise the character lacks pressure, and the reader feels it.

**Secondary characters see the protagonist differently.**
That is their most important contribution. They show sides the protagonist does not see in himself: a secondary character who takes him for strong when he feels weak, or for cold when he believes he is opening up. That creates depth without explanation.

**They speak differently.**
Every character has a different rhythm, different vocabulary, different subjects he comes back to. When all the characters sound like the protagonist, it is not an ensemble but a monologue in costumes.

**Introduce sparingly, carry through consistently.**
Better a few secondary characters who stay in the memory than many who blur. Scribophile calls it the "economy of characters": every newly introduced character costs the reader mental energy. That energy has to be worth spending.

**No existing purely in the service of the plot.**
Secondary characters who only appear when the protagonist needs them work like props. A character who lives a life of his own, even if it is only hinted at, feels real.

*Sources: Scribophile, "Secondary Characters: What They Are, and How to Create Unforgettable Ones" (scribophile.com); DabbleWriter, "Craft Intriguing Secondary Characters in Exactly 7 Steps" (dabblewriter.com)*

## Motivators (Mentor Figures)

Motivators are characters whose main function is to keep the protagonist moving: mentors, confidants, moral counterweights. That is a dramaturgical role, not a license for flatness.

**The motivator is not an oracle.**
In *The Writer's Journey* (1992) Christopher Vogler describes the mentor as an archetype who hands over wisdom and equipment, but he stresses: the mentor sends the hero off, he does not go along. Anyone who tells the protagonist what to do and is always right is a crutch, not a character. The motivator may be wrong. May hold outdated knowledge. May pursue an agenda of his own.

**A wound of his own, a history of his own.**
K.M. Weiland writes at helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com: the most effective mentors have an unresolved question or a failure of their own in their past that colors their advice. That makes their guidance ambiguous and so makes it interesting.

**Mentors give tools, not answers.**
The strongest mentor function: they give the protagonist the tool with which he can find the answer *himself*. A mentor who supplies the answer prevents the character arc.

**An exit with consequences.**
Many motivators die or disappear in the second act. That is dramaturgically legitimate, but only if the exit really does leave the protagonist alone, if the crutch goes and he has to stand without it. Whoever removes the motivator early should make sure the loss is still felt in act III.

**No constant praise.**
Motivators who confirm the protagonist without pause create no tension. They are worth more when they also say the uncomfortable truths he does not always want to hear.

*Sources: Christopher Vogler, The Writer's Journey (1992, 3rd ed. 2007); K.M. Weiland, "4 Ways to Write a Thought-Provoking Mentor Character" (helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com); Self-Publishing School, "Craft the Mentor Archetype in 7 Steps" (self-publishingschool.com)*

## The Antagonist

The antagonist is the protagonist's most important mirror. Write him as an obstacle and you give away half his effect.

**The antagonist believes he is in the right.**
This is not optional, it is the basic condition. Brandon Sanderson puts it this way in his writing lectures (2025): if you want an antagonist whose motivation the reader can follow, you give him realistic motives just as you do the protagonist, and then you set him on a collision course. An antagonist who knows he is doing evil and does it anyway is a villain. What is interesting is the man who is convinced that what he does is necessary.

**Know the best version of his argument.**
Before writing the antagonist you should be able to formulate his position convincingly, without irony, without hidden disapproval. If the antagonist only ever sounds wrong, he is no threat. If he *might* be right, he is dangerous.

**Sanderson's scales.**
Sanderson describes antagonists on the same three axes as protagonists: likability, proactivity, competence. An iconic antagonist like Sauron is low on likability but high on competence and proactivity. A more complex antagonist like Gollum moves on all three scales and changes. Which type fits depends on the story.

**A balance of power.**
The antagonist has to be able to threaten the protagonist seriously: physically, emotionally or through revelation. He can beat the protagonist, and sometimes he does. An antagonist who never wins is not an antagonist.

**Parallel wounds, opposite conclusions.**
The strongest construction: protagonist and antagonist have similar starting points, similar losses, similar experiences, but have drawn opposite conclusions from them. That makes the confrontation more than a fight. It becomes the question of who is right.

**Present even in absence.**
The antagonist does not have to appear in every scene to have an effect. When his decisions, his systems, his shadow are palpable in the protagonist's world, he is present. That is often more effective than frequent direct encounters.

*Sources: Brandon Sanderson, Writing Lectures #5 and #6 (brandonsanderson.com, 2025); YouTube: "Writing Realistic Villain Motivation" (2025); Sanderson's character scales, September C. Fawkes (septembercfawkes.com)*

## Character-Arc Types

Not every character changes. K.M. Weiland distinguishes three basic forms, which differ not in quality but in function.

**Positive arc (change arc).** The character starts in a lie about himself and arrives at a truth. The classic hero: fearful and selfish at the beginning, brave and connected at the end. The most common form and therefore the most worn out.

**Negative arc (fall arc).** The character knows the truth, rejects it, sinks deeper into the lie. Macbeth, Walter White, Anakin Skywalker. Dramatically satisfying when the fall follows from consistent character traits and not from outward accident.

**Flat arc (steadfast arc).** The character knows the truth from the start and changes *the world around him* instead of changing himself. Atticus Finch, Captain America, Mary Poppins. Common in mentor roles and in series heroes who have to stay constant across many volumes.

Which arc fits depends on the plot. Coming-of-age stories need positive arcs, tragedies negative ones, mentors and sequels often carry flat arcs.

*Source: K.M. Weiland, Creating Character Arcs (2016)*

## The Lie the Character Believes

The working form of want versus need. The character believes something false about himself, the world or what would make him happy. That lie is not stupidity but a protective construction against the ghost.

Examples:
- "I must not trust anyone." (after a betrayal in the past)
- "I am only worth something when I am useful." (after a loveless childhood)
- "Strength means needing nobody." (after a loss)

The plot puts that lie under pressure. In the positive arc the character learns that the lie is untenable. In the negative arc he defends it into ruin. In the flat arc he is the only one in the book who does *not* believe the lie, and he confronts others with the truth.

The lie never has to be spoken. It is recognizable in behavior: in what the character avoids, in the relationships he sabotages, in the compliments he cannot accept.

*Source: K.M. Weiland, helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com*

## Character Voice

Every character sounds different. Voice is the sum of vocabulary, syntax, subjects and avoidances.

**Vocabulary.** Academics reach for technical terms, craftsmen for the concrete names of tools, soldiers for abbreviations. A man who says "interesting" all the time is not the man who says "passable".

**Syntax.** Short main clauses sound different from nested constructions. A character who never speaks a subordinate clause comes across differently from one who is constantly framing hypotheses in the subjunctive.

**Subjects.** What does the character talk about again and again? What does he come back to against his will? That is often the ghost leaking through the language.

**Avoidances.** Just as important: what does the character *never* talk about? Which words does he not use? A character who never says the word "love" characterizes himself through that gap.

Test: three lines of dialogue without speaker tags, assigned by a reader who knows the book. If he cannot assign them, the characters sound the same.

## Foil Characters

A foil is a character who makes a trait of the protagonist visible through contrast. The antagonist is a special case, but foils can also be allies or secondary characters.

Examples:
- Watson to Holmes: shows how unusually Holmes's mind works.
- Hermione to Ron: shows discipline against looseness, both against Harry's middle ground.
- Mercutio to Romeo: shows cynical distance from romance against romantic abandon.

Foils are not mirrors but contrasts along a single axis. A foil who is the opposite in every respect becomes a caricature. One who is clearly opposed on one axis and similar on others sharpens the protagonist without duplicating him.

## Backstory Drip-Feeding

The ghost only works while it stays partly covered. Full disclosure early in the book takes the wound's power away.

**Hint before explaining.** A reaction too strong for the situation, before the reader knows the reason. Then later, in passing, the triggering event.

**In fragments.** One memory in act I, a second in act II, the missing piece only shortly before the climax. The reader builds the picture himself, and that is part of his bond with the character.

**Not as an explanatory monologue.** The temptation to spread the ghost out in a quiet scene is strong. It almost always weakens the book. Better: another character asks a question that goes unanswered. A place is avoided. A name sets off a reaction that hangs in the room.

**Late completeness.** Some stories never reveal the ghost entirely. That is legitimate when the emotional truth is clear and only the facts stay vague.

## Quick Reference: The Five Roles

| Role | Core question | Common mistake |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Protagonist** | What does he want, and what does he really need? | Want and need are identical |
| **Love interest** | Does she have a life without the protagonist? | Exists only as a reward or a mirror |
| **Secondary characters** | What do they want when the protagonist is not watching? | Appear only when needed |
| **Motivator** | May he be wrong? Does he have a wound of his own? | Always gives the right answer |
| **Antagonist** | Does he believe he is in the right? | Is evil because the story needs a villain |
