# Planning Methods & Plot Structures

---

## The Snowflake Method
*Developed by Randy Ingermanson (physicist and author)*

The story is built out in 10 steps from a single sentence into a complete novel, like a snowflake growing from a simple pattern into a complex structure.

**The 10 steps:**

1. **Core sentence:** Sum up the whole story in one sentence. That forces you to think the heart of the story through clearly.
2. **Paragraph:** Expand the sentence into 5 sentences: starting situation, disaster 1, disaster 2, disaster 3, ending.
3. **Character summaries:** Write a one-page summary for every main character: goal, motivation, conflict, development.
4. **Expanded plot summary:** Build the paragraph from step 2 out to about one page.
5. **Character arcs:** For every important character, describe his complete course through the story.
6. **Four-page summary:** Expand each disaster scene from step 2 onto a page of its own.
7. **Detailed character profiles:** Write full profiles for all relevant characters.
8. **Scene list:** Draw up a table of all scenes with content, most important character, perspective, goal of the scene.
9. **Scene notes:** For every scene write a short description from the character's point of view.
10. **First draft:** Now write the novel.

**Suited to:** plotters, first-time authors, anyone who needs structure in order not to get stuck.

**Source:** Randy Ingermanson, *How to Write a Novel Using the Snowflake Method* (2014); ingermanson.com

## The Hero's Journey (Monomyth)
*Concept by Joseph Campbell, popularized by Christopher Vogler ("The Writer's Journey")*

The oldest and most universal narrative pattern there is, traceable in myths, fairy tales and modern blockbusters.

**The 12 stages:**

1. **The ordinary world:** the hero in his everyday life, before anything begins.
2. **The call to adventure:** an event that tears the hero out of his comfort zone.
3. **The refusal:** the hero hesitates, doubts, turns the call down at first.
4. **The mentor:** a figure gives the hero advice, equipment or courage.
5. **Crossing the first threshold:** the hero enters the other world, with no way back.
6. **Tests, allies, enemies:** the hero gets to know the new world.
7. **The innermost cave:** the hero approaches the core of his greatest fear.
8. **The ordeal:** the turning point. The hero "dies" symbolically and is reborn.
9. **The reward:** the hero takes what he fought for.
10. **The road back:** the consequences of the decision follow.
11. **The resurrection:** the final test. The hero has to use everything he has learned.
12. **Return with the elixir:** the hero comes back changed and brings something with him.

**Note on use:** The hero's journey is a *metastructure model*, not a rigid scheme. It describes emotional and character development and can be combined with the three-act structure.

**Sources:** Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949); Christopher Vogler, *The Writer's Journey* (1992, 3rd ed. 2007)

## The Three-Act Structure
*Classical dramaturgy since Aristotle*

The simplest and most robust plot structure:

| Act | Share | Content |
|-----|--------|--------|
| **Act I: setup** | ~25% | Establish world, characters, conflict. Ends with the *inciting incident*. |
| **Act II: confrontation** | ~50% | Escalation of the conflict, turning points, midpoint crisis. The hero is at his lowest. |
| **Act III: resolution** | ~25% | Climax, decision, consequences, epilogue. |

**The most important structural points:**
- **Plot point 1** (end of act I): the situation changes fundamentally.
- **Pinch point 1** (about 37%): a moment of pressure that makes the antagonist's threat concrete.
- **Midpoint** (middle of act II): apparent victory or total collapse, which changes the direction.
- **Pinch point 2** (about 62%): a second moment of pressure that raises the threat and sharpens the line towards plot point 2.
- **Plot point 2** (end of act II): the darkest moment. The hero has to make a fundamental decision.
- **Climax** (act III): everything comes together. The hero wins or fails, but he is not the same any more.

**Source:** Syd Field, *Screenplay: The Foundations of Screenwriting* (1979), the classic formalization of the three-act structure for modern storytelling.

## The W-Question Model (Plotting with Core Questions)

Answer these questions before writing:

- **WHO** is the protagonist? What does he want? What does he fear?
- **WHAT** is at stake? (inner and outer conflict)
- **WHY** now? What set the story off?
- **HOW** does the hero try to reach his goal?
- **WHAT** makes him fail? Which obstacles?
- **WHERE** does the road lead? What is the story saying?

## Pantsing vs. Plotting

| Method | Description | Famous practitioners |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Plotting** (plotter) | Detailed plan before writing | J.K. Rowling, Randy Ingermanson |
| **Pantsing** (pantser) | Writing intuitively, by the seat of the pants | Stephen King, George R.R. Martin (formerly) |
| **Plantser** | Rough structure plus intuitive writing | Many successful authors |

## Midpoint: The Most Important Structural Point

The midpoint is not simply the middle. It is the place where the story changes its direction of travel. Without a strong midpoint, act II collapses in the middle. That is the most common place for manuscripts to run aground.

**Three functions:**

1. **Mirror midpoint.** A scene that shows the opening reflected. The same situation, but the hero can no longer be the same man. It makes the change so far visible.
2. **False victory.** The hero believes he has won. The trap: what he has won was not the right thing. From here the plot tips.
3. **False defeat.** The hero believes he has lost everything. He gets up off the floor a different man, with new knowledge, new resolve, often a new identity.

The midpoint separates the reactive from the proactive phase: before it the hero reacts, after it he acts. That shift is the real content of the arc.

*Source: Michael Hauge, Writing Screenplays That Sell (1988); the Save the Cat tradition, Blake Snyder*

## Try-Fail Cycles: Yes, but / No, and

A method from Brandon Sanderson's writing lectures. Every scene ends in one of four ways, three of which drive the plot:

| Outcome | Effect | Example |
|----------|---------|---------|
| **Yes** | Hero reaches the goal, tension drops | "He found the key." |
| **Yes, but** | Hero reaches the goal, but a new complication | "He found the key, but the lock had been changed." |
| **No** | Hero fails, no movement | "He did not find the key." |
| **No, and** | Hero fails, and the situation gets worse | "He did not find the key, and the guards are coming closer." |

**Yes** and **No** as a scene outcome are dead. **Yes, but** and **No, and** keep the pressure on. In most novels a chain of "No, and / No, and / Yes, but" runs up to the climax, where the first real "Yes" stands, the one the story has been looking for.

*Source: Brandon Sanderson, Writing Lectures #4 (brandonsanderson.com)*

## Subplots

A novel without subplots is a sketch. Subplots give the world depth, the protagonist surfaces to be reflected in, and the pacing variation.

**Connection to the main plot.** A subplot with nothing to do with the main plot is a second story in the same book. The connection can be thematic (same question, different answer), figural (a secondary character whose decision forces the protagonist's hand) or causal (a subplot event becomes a lever in the plot).

**An arc of its own.** Every subplot has its own setup, confrontation and resolution. If the subplot has no arc of its own, it is not a subplot but a side thread.

**Romantic subplots.** The most common kind. They work when the romantic tension sharpens the main conflict: the character has to choose between the relationship and the task, or the relationship forces him to see himself anew.

**Resolved before or with the main plot.** Subplots are closed before or during the climax, never after it. A subplot closed after the climax drains energy.

**Introduce them sparingly.** Three subplots are enough for most novels. More dilute the main plot.

## Setup & Payoff

Chekhov's gun as a structural principle, not as an anecdote.

**Setup.** An element is introduced in passing: an object, an ability, a piece of information, a relationship. In passing means not highlighted, not explained, not overemphasized. The reader takes it in without storing it.

**Payoff.** Later the element becomes decisive. The reader recognizes it, often with a quiet "ah, so that was it". That recognition is one of the strongest rewards a novel can give.

**Three rules:**

- **Setup before payoff, always.** An element explained only when it is used is deus ex machina.
- **Set up twice, not three times.** Once and the reader may miss it, twice and he notices. Three times is manipulation, and the reader sees the payoff coming.
- **Scatter false payoffs.** Not every setup has to be redeemed. Some setups are pure world detail. A novel in which every element has a function feels mechanical.

**The inverse principle.** When a dramatic moment in the plot feels unearned, what is usually missing is the setup before it, not the moment itself.
